Three directions, one screen

Each of these is the home studio holding identical content — the same couple, the same six tools, the same staging panel, the same status strip. Only colour, structure and typography change, so what you are comparing is the thing you objected to and nothing else.

The stage in the middle is a CSS stand-in, not the real render. It is there to show how each direction frames the view and how the chrome sits beside a warm image.

Three things worth judging, because they are what separate the options:

  • 1Can you tell chrome from artwork? Today the primary button is the same colour as the pews and the gold token is byte-identical to the brass in the render. A control has to be categorically different from the thing it controls.
  • 2Does the church look lit, or printed? Today the interface is 40–55 lightness points brighter than the render, so the 3D reads as an insert on paper rather than a stage with the lights on.
  • 3Do the panels have edges or gutters? Today every box is a 1px outline with a 10px gap around it, because the card fill measures 1.11:1 against the page and is invisible. Outlines floating in gutters is how a spread is built.
warm-instrument

Vellum & Ink

Keep the cream the owner chose from his own reference, but stop treating it as paper — drain the tint to near-neutral, cut one dark well into it for the render, and let the green become the darkest mass on the page rather than a colour that competes with the pews.

The honest risk. This is the fifth attempt and it looks, at a glance, like the first one. The owner approved warm cream from his own reference and then said the result looks like a magazine — if what he is actually reacting to is the cream itself rather than its execution, this direction hands him a lower-chroma, better-engineered version of the thing he just rejected, and the sixth rejection costs more than the fifth. Two specific weaknesses to own: (1) the accent is still 1° of hue from the pews at #3c4a33, separated only by 9 L* points — the argument that the dark well is a moat holds only as long as no green chrome is ever placed inside or adjacent to the stage, and that is a discipline, not a mechanism; (2) at C 1.8 the canvas still discounts some of the render's warmth through illuminant adaptation, so the church will look marginally less golden here than in either of the other two. This is the direction with the smallest diff, the cheapest print story, and the least new information about what the owner actually wants.

petrol-bicameral

Cold Room, Warm Stage

The application is a cool, neutral, paper-white document and the render is a dark lit well cut into it — two chambers with a 16:1 cliff between them, so the chrome can never be mistaken for the artwork and the artwork gets warmer just by being surrounded by something that isn't.

The honest risk. Nothing on screen is warm, and nothing on screen says wedding. A cool grey document with a petrol button is unmistakably an instrument, and that is the brief — but the owner has now rejected a near-monochrome direction once as "too sterile", and although this is cool-neutral rather than warm-grey, has three real planes rather than a flat field, and has an unambiguous accent where that attempt had none, it is adjacent enough to the rejection that it should be prototyped on the live deploy and looked at for a day before it is committed to. The second risk is register: petrol at C 7.5 on cool grey is the palette of a colour-grading suite, and a couple arriving to plan the most emotional day of their lives meets a machine. The counter-argument is that the emotion is supposed to live in the render, and this is the only one of the three that makes the render measurably more beautiful — but it is a real bet, and if the owner wants the chrome itself to feel like a wedding, this is the wrong one.

graphite-ledger

The Graphite Room

Turn the lights off in the room and leave them on in the church — a dark neutral shell where the render is the only light source, all chrome is dark glass floating on the image itself, and the type is chosen so that a forgotten declaration cannot make a column of numbers wobble.

The honest risk. A dark luxe theme was already built here and abandoned back to light. This is a better-reasoned version — neutral rather than moody, chroma-disciplined at C ≤ 1.5 on every chrome token, with a verdigris that has a material argument rather than a vibe — but it is unmistakably the same direction, and if the earlier rejection was about darkness itself rather than its execution, this gets rejected too and the fifth iteration burns on a re-run. Three further risks worth naming honestly. (1) Overlay chrome stops being calm past a certain control count, and this studio has six rail tools plus a 320px inspector; a floating panel of Swedish labels over a moving render is a legibility problem you have to actively defend, shot by shot. (2) The inspector-as-overlay covers the right third of the frame, which is where the couple stands — that needs either a camera offset when the panel opens or an inspector that docks. (3) Print parity is the only genuinely expensive engineering item across all three directions, and it lives entirely here. The upside is proportional: this is the only direction where the render is unambiguously the product, it is the only one that answers "stop looking like a magazine" at the very first pixel, and it is the only one whose typography makes a wobbling number column structurally impossible.

What picking one actually commits to

  • It is not a token swap. :root defines 51 custom properties, but the stylesheet carries another 74 hex and 394 rgba literals outside that block, plus about 25 UI colours sitting in components. Re-pointing the tokens alone leaves roughly 470 values behind — which is why earlier attempts looked half-finished.
  • The 3D keeps its own colours. The 204 hex values in the church scene are materials — mahogany, limestone, brass. They are not chrome and they do not change.
  • Two text tokens are below the accessibility floor today. --muted measures 3.86:1 and --subtle 2.46:1, and they carry 161 of the stylesheet's colour declarations. All three directions fix this; it is not optional.
  • The palette lock in CLAUDE.md gets rewritten in the same commit as the change. A lock is only useful while it is true.
